\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

In nature, species coexist and interact within complex communities, yet whether these assemblages function as cohesive, integrated units or merely as loose collections of independently acting species remains a fundamental question in ecology. Historically, this tension has been framed through two influential paradigms. Clements’ “superorganism” view treats communities as discrete biological entities with emergent properties arising from species interdependencies, potentially as a result of coevolution\citep{Clements1916, Clements1936, Odum1969, Lovelock1979, WilsonSober1989}. In contrast, Gleason’s individualistic hypothesis posits that species independently occupy niches, with community composition emerging from the coincidental overlap of species ranges\citep{Gleason1939, Cain1947, Mason1947, Whittaker1967}. This classical contrast provides a useful lens for ecological research questions. For instance, the holistic view motivates the study of interaction networks and emergent dynamics\citep{Dunne2002, Allison2008}, while the individualistic view directs attention to species-level functional traits and environmental filtering\citep{Keddy1992, McGill2006}. Despite decades of research, the conditions that determine when communities behave as cohesive units versus loose species assemblages remain unclear.

These contrasting frameworks make distinct predictions in the context of community coalescence, the mixing of previously isolated communities\citep{Rillig2015, Lechon2021}. Coalescence occurs across diverse contexts and scales: environmental disturbances trigger microbial community mixing in soils\citep{Huet2023, Bresciani2025}, flooding events promote coalescence in aquatic and estuarine habitats\citep{Liu2024}, skin microbiomes undergo exchange through daily social contact\citep{Sarkar2024}, and gut microbiomes are subject to complete community transfer through fecal microbiota transplantation\citep{Xiao2020, Gupta2016}. Coalescence brings species with distinct interaction histories into a shared ecological arena, creating novel cross-community interactions that can reshape the resulting assemblage\citep{Rillig2015}. Thus, it provides a natural test of whether outcomes are determined collectively or by individual species. The holistic view predicts differential persistence of communities as integrated units, producing asymmetric outcomes where one community dominates\citep{Castledine2020, Sierocinski2017}---a phenomenon we term community-level selection (CLS). The individualistic view predicts species-level selection, yielding mixtures shaped by individual fitness regardless of community origin.

Substantial theoretical and empirical evidence supports the holistic prediction of community-level selection during coalescence. Early theoretical work by Gilpin\citep{Gilpin1994} showed that pre-assembled communities, having already undergone internal competitive exclusion, possess structured interaction patterns that differ fundamentally from randomly assembled communities. Because species within such communities have been filtered to coexist, their survival outcomes become coupled, producing asymmetric post-coalescence communities dominated by one parent\citep{Debray2022}. Notably, this structured interaction can arise due to ecological exclusion alone, even in the absence of long-term coevolution.  Building on this foundation, resource-consumer models formalized the mechanism by which such selection occurs, demonstrating that communities with superior collective fitness through resource consumption outcompete others in ways not predictable from individual species performance alone\citep{Tikhonov2016, Lechon2021}. Empirically, co-selection between dominant and subdominant taxa has been observed across 100 coalescence experiments, confirming that species retention within communities is indeed correlated\citep{DiazColunga2022}. 

However, opposing evidence supports the individualistic prediction that species respond independently to coalescence events, with outcomes primarily determined by environmental sorting rather than community-level competition. Empirical work reports that coexistence between species from different source communities is common through niche partitioning rather than competitive exclusion during macro-scale biogeographic interchange in marine and terrestrial biomes\citep{Vermeij1991}, and similar patterns have been observed in microbial systems where closely related species coexist via resource partitioning\citep{Zheng2021}. Other work shows that species from the same community rarely go extinct together in the fossil record\citep{BentonEmerson2007}. In microbial systems, local environmental factors explain more variation than the presence of constantly interchanging neighboring communities\citep{VanderGucht2007}. Strain-resolved metagenomic analyses of \textit{in vitro} gut microbial communities %grown in rich media 
showed species-level rather than community-level selection, with surviving species originating from both parent communities\citep{Goldman2025, Walton2025}. These findings suggest that selection during coalescence acts on species-level traits rather than on emergent community properties.

Together, these contrasting lines of evidence indicate that community-level selection occurs in some coalescence events but not others---yet the conditions that determine when communities behave as cohesive units remain poorly understood. Prior studies have documented outcomes consistent with either view, but have not systematically identified the factors that govern which outcome arises. Here, we investigate how interspecies interaction strength governs the transition between these contrasting outcomes. We demonstrate that when interactions are moderate to strong, communities behave as integrated units subject to selection; when interactions are weak, species respond independently, and community-level selection largely disappears. We classify post-coalescence outcomes into three types based on their similarity to parental communities: CLS, where one parent overwhelms the other; Mixture, where both parents persist at comparable levels; and Restructuring, where a novel state emerges distinct from both parents. Using a generalized Lotka--Volterra model with random pairwise interactions, we show that interaction strength alone predicts the transition from Mixture to CLS. Experiments manipulating nutrient concentration confirm this prediction, and these patterns generalize to natural sample-derived communities. Finally, we identify two distinct regimes underlying CLS: an emergent regime at intermediate interaction strength, where collective dynamics shape outcomes, and a species-driven regime at strong interactions, where dominant species determine the winner.
