\subsection{Community-level selection is prevalent in microbial coalescence}
\label{sec:results:cls_prevalent}

During community coalescence, a central question is whether selection acts primarily on individual species or on whole communities as cohesive units. To address this, we asked how often coalescence outcomes resemble one parental community versus blending both or forming a novel community. Consider a coalescence event where parental communities A and B mix to produce community C (\figref{fig:fig1}A). We represent each community by its abundance vector and compute cosine similarity between C and each parent (\figref{fig:fig1}B):
\begin{equation}
\text{Sim}(C, A) = \vec{x}_C \cdot \vec{x}_A, \quad \text{Sim}(C, B) = \vec{x}_C \cdot \vec{x}_B
\label{eq:similarity}
\end{equation}
where $\vec{x}_A$, $\vec{x}_B$ are L2-normalized abundance vectors of parents A and B, and $\vec{x}_C$ is that of the coalesced community. These two similarity scores yield a two-dimensional similarity map in which the location of C reflects how strongly it resembles each parent. We partition this map into three classes, in which each corresponds to distinct hypotheses about the unit of selection. If C lies close to one parent and far from the other, we infer community-level selection (CLS) for that parental community. If C lies near the diagonal and similarly resembles both parents, it is consistent with a Mixture where both parental communities coexist. If C lies far from both parents, the community has reassembled into a novel configuration, which we term Restructuring. Precise geometric boundaries are given in the Methods.

To quantify these outcome types experimentally, we assembled random in-vitro communities and subjected them to pairwise coalescence (\figref{fig:fig1}C). We first curated a strain library of 54 bacterial isolates collected from diverse environments (soil, tree, and flower pods). The library is phylogenetically broad, spanning 29 families across three phyla: Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, and Bacteroidota (Supplementary Fig.~S1, S2). From this library, we assembled 42 parental communities with varying initial richness (9 with 6 species, 9 with 12 species, and 12 with 24 species; each with two replicates) and stabilized them for 7 days under daily growth--dilution cycles ($\times$30 every 24~h) (\figref{fig:fig1}C). Our initial experiments were done in Base medium (BM) (1~g~L$^{-1}$ yeast extract, 1~g~L$^{-1}$ soytone, 10~mM sodium phosphate, 0.1~mM CaCl$_2$, 2~mM MgCl$_2$, 4~mg~L$^{-1}$ NiSO$_4$, 50~mg~L$^{-1}$ MnCl$_2$, 5~g~L$^{-1}$ glucose and 4~g~L$^{-1}$ urea; pH~6.5), a buffered complex medium that supports high species coexistence (Supplementary Information, mean species survival ratio = $74 \pm 2$\%). We then performed 83 pairwise coalescence events by mixing parents at equal volume and restabilizing for an additional 7 days. Community composition of parental communities and coalesced communities were measured at the end of stabilization by 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing (ASV-level profiles), and we also recorded the biomass (OD) and pH of the communities to contextualize assembly and post-coalescence dynamics.

Representative time series illustrate the spectrum of post-coalescence dynamics (\figref{fig:fig1}D). In outcomes classified as CLS, one parental lineage rapidly displaces the other after mixing and the coalesced community converges to a composition closely matching that parent, while largely preserving its internal relative-abundance structure over serial dilution cycles. This behaviour is consistent with selection acting at the community level rather than on individual species alone. In the Restructuring case, the merged community converges toward a novel state distinct from both parental communities. Among 12 randomly selected time-series trajectories, we did not observe Mixture cases in which both parents remained comparably represented after coalescence (see Supplementary Fig.~S3).

Projecting all outcomes into the similarity space (\figref{fig:fig1}E) revealed that CLS is the most frequent empirical outcome. Of the 82 analyzable coalescence events (one excluded due to low sequencing depth), 54 were classified as CLS, 26 as Restructuring, and only 2 as Mixture (\figref{fig:fig1}E). Thus, under our experimental condition, most coalescence events end with one community effectively excluding the other and determining the final composition. This pattern was robust across variants of similarity metrics (Supplementary Fig.~S4). We asked whether the high frequency of CLS could be explained by chance given skewed abundance distributions that overweight high-abundant species. As a control, we built a null model in which species were retained independently with uniform probability matching to the observed richness (Methods). The observed selection preference between the retentions of the two parents during coalescence far exceeded this null expectation (Supplementary Fig.~S5), indicating that community-level selection reflects genuine ecological dynamics beyond statistical artifacts of the metric.

\subsection{Theoretical model with Random gLV interactions reproduce community-level selection}
\label{sec:results:glv_model}

Community-level selection (CLS) cannot arise if communities are simply non-interacting collections of species sampled from a pool, as shown by the null model in the previous section. To test whether generic interspecies interactions are sufficient to generate CLS, we built a generalized Lotka--Volterra (gLV) model that mirrors the experimental protocol (\figref{fig:fig2}A). In the gLV model, given species $i \in \{1, 2, 3, \ldots N\}$, the dynamics are given as:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dn_i}{dt} = n_i \left( r_i - \sum_j \alpha_{ij} n_j \right)
\label{eq:glv}
\end{equation}
where $n_i$ is the abundance of species $i$ and $r_i$ is the intrinsic growth rate set uniformly to 1. We set self-regulation $\alpha_{ii} = 1$ and drew off-diagonal competition coefficients $\alpha_{ij} \geq 0$ independently from $\text{Uniform}(0, 2\mu)$ with mean interaction strength $\mu = \langle \alpha_{ij} \rangle$; increasing $\mu$ strengthens the net competitive interactions between species. From a species pool of $N = 48$, we generated two parental communities by sampling non-overlapping species subsets (size $S=12$ each), initialized abundances log-uniformly between $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-2}$, and evolved each to steady state (extinction threshold $10^{-4}$; stability defined by $\left|\frac{dn_i}{dt}\right| < 10^{-6}$ over the final window). Coalescence was simulated by equally mixing two equilibrated parental communities, then evolved to the new steady state (\figref{fig:fig2}A). The steady state abudnacen is noramlized by total abundance and used for similarity calculations as in the experiment.

We performed simulations at interaction strength $\mu = 0.6$ with 200 replicates, analyzing the cosine similarity of each coalescence outcome to the two parental communities. At this interaction strength, the random-interaction model quantitatively reproduces the high frequency of CLS observed in the experiments. The distribution of outcomes in the similarity map concentrates near the A-like or B-like axes, indicating that coalescence outcomes C typically resemble one parental community rather than forming symmetric mixtures (\figref{fig:fig2}B). Quantitatively, CLS accounts for 59.1\% of all outcomes, far more frequent than Restructuring (21.6\%) or Mixture (19.2\%; \figref{fig:fig2}B, right). This observation suggests that interspecies interactions alone, a minimal and generic condition, are sufficient to generate community-level selection. 

To further understand how CLS emerges from random interactions, we investigated the role of the assembly process of the two parental communities. During assembly, species compete and some go extinct; those that survive to coexistence tend to have weaker mutual interactions on average. We quantified this assembly effect by comparing the mean pairwise interaction strength $\langle \alpha_{ij} \rangle$ among species before versus after assembly (\figref{fig:fig2}C). Assembled communities exhibit significantly reduced mean interaction strength compared with the initial random draw (paired $t$-test, $p < 0.001$), indicating that assembly selectively retains species with less competitive (weaker) interactions. This assembly effect creates internal coherence within each parental community: species that ``survived together'' share a history of less competitive interactions. When two such communities coalesce, we therefore expect species from the same parent to have correlated fates: if one survives, its co-assembled partners are also likely to survive. To test this, we computed pairwise selection correlations: for each pair of species, we asked whether their retention probabilities across coalescence events were correlated (\figref{fig:fig2}D). Species pairs from the same parental community showed strongly positive selection correlation, whereas species pairs drawn from different parents showed weak or negative correlation. Crucially, this pattern appears in both simulation and experiment (\figref{fig:fig2}D), indicating that the assembly-driven mechanism can account for CLS in real communities. Overall, these results show that community-level selection can arise without invoking any particular structure in the interspecies interaction network, because all pairwise interactions are drawn independently from a simple random distribution.

\subsection{Interaction strength controls the occurrence of community-level selection}
\label{sec:results:interaction_strength}

Having established that interspecies interactions can generate CLS, we next asked whether interaction strength alone could predict coalescence outcomes. We simulated 1,000 coalescence events at each of three representative interaction strengths ($\mu = 0.3, 0.6, 0.8$) and mapped outcomes into the similarity space (\figref{fig:fig3}A). At weak interactions ($\mu = 0.3$), outcomes clustered in the interior of the map, indicating that both parental communities persisted comparably after mixing. As $\mu$ increased to 0.6 and 0.8, outcomes shifted toward the axes, indicating that one parent increasingly dominated the other.

To quantify this shift, we defined the Selection Preference Ratio (SPR), which measures how strongly an outcome favors one parent over the other:
\begin{equation}
\text{SPR} = \frac{\left| \arctan\left(\frac{\text{Sim}(C,B)}{\text{Sim}(C,A)}\right) - \frac{\pi}{4} \right|}{\pi/4}
\label{eq:spr}
\end{equation}
Geometrically, SPR measures the normalized angular deviation from the diagonal $\text{Sim}(C,A) = \text{Sim}(C,B)$; SPR $= 0$ corresponds to balanced contributions from both parents, while SPR $= 1$ corresponds to complete preference for one parent. At $\mu = 0.3$, SPR values concentrated near zero, consistent with Mixture-dominated outcomes. At $\mu = 0.6$ and $0.8$, SPR distributions became bimodal and skewed toward 1, reflecting more frequent and stronger CLS (\figref{fig:fig3}A, histograms).

Aggregating outcomes across $\mu = 0$--$1.2$ revealed a monotonic regime shift (\figref{fig:fig3}B). At low $\mu$, Mixture was the most frequent outcome and CLS was rare. As $\mu$ increased, the Mixture fraction declined while CLS rose and eventually dominated. Restructuring emerged only at the highest interaction strengths. These patterns were robust to variation in growth rates, carrying capacities, interaction distributions, and similarity metrics (Supplementary Figs.~S6--S8). Together, the simulations predict that tuning interaction strength should shift coalescence outcomes from Mixture toward CLS, a hypothesis we test experimentally in the next section.

\subsection{Nutrient-dependent interaction strength in experiments recapitulates model predictions}
\label{sec:results:nutrient_experiment}

We asked whether the model-predicted shift in coalescence outcomes depending on interaction strength can be recapitulated experimentally, in particular whether decreasing interaction strength would move coalescence outcomes from CLS toward Mixture. Following prior work \citep{Hu2022, Ratzke2020b, Hu2025}, we used nutrient concentration as a manipulable control knob for interaction strength. To establish this gradient, we prepared three media that differ in carbon/nitrogen availability (glucose, urea) (\figref{fig:fig4}A): Nutr$-$ (low), Base (moderate), and Nutr+ (high). Higher carbon and nitrogen availability is expected to steepen consumer--resource feedbacks and intensify environmental modification, thereby increasing the interaction between microbial species \citep{Ratzke2020b}. To empirically ground this assumption, we measured the failed invasion frequency across all pairwise combinations of the 12 most abundant isolates using 95:5 initial-frequency invasion assays (Methods, Supplementary Fig.~S8--S10). The fraction of failed invasions increased monotonically with nutrient supply (\figref{fig:fig4}B), consistent with stronger competitive interactions under enrichment. Using this metric to calibrate the gLV simulations yielded approximate mean interaction strengths of $\mu < 0.5$ for Nutr$-$, $\mu \simeq 0.72$ for Base, and $\mu \simeq 0.92$ for Nutr+ (calibration procedure in Supplementary Methods).

We next examined how coalescence outcomes change across these three media with different strengths of interaction. We performed the coalescence experiment with three media by constructing the parental communities and coalesced communities with the same subset of microbial libraries used in \figref{fig:fig1}. The geometry of outcomes in the similarity map showed a clear dependence on nutrient concentration (\figref{fig:fig4}C). Under low concentration (Nutr$-$), points clustered away from the axes with low dominance scores, indicating that both parental lineages tended to persist and mixing was common. At the intermediate concentration (Base), the distribution stretched toward one axis, reflecting more frequent dominance events, while a subset of points fell into the low-similarity sector consistent with restructuring. At high concentration (Nutr+), points concentrated near the axes, and dominance scores peaked near 0 or 1, signifying stronger selection preference and more consistent dominance by one parent, with rare restructuring. We therefore observe little community-level selection in Nutr$-$ media where there are weak interspecies interaction, as predicted by the Lotka-Volterra model.

The outcome fractions shifted systematically with nutrient concentration as predicted by the model (\figref{fig:fig4}D). In Nutr$-$ media, approximately 34\% of events were classified as CLS, 56\% as Mixture, and 10\% as Restructuring. In Base media, CLS rose to about 60\%, Mixture declined to 8\%, and Restructuring increased to 32\%. In Nutr+ media, CLS further increased to 72\%, Restructuring declined to 21\%, and Mixture accounted for the remainder. Quantitatively, these shifts match the simulation-derived prediction that interaction strength strengthens the occurrence of CLS and Restructuring. In summary, the results support our prediction that higher nutrient concentration strengthens interspecies interactions and thereby intensifies the degree of community-level selection, steering outcomes away from Mixture and toward CLS and Restructuring.

\subsection{Predictability of community-level selection separates into two regimes}
\label{sec:results:predictability}

We have observed community-level selection (CLS) in both Base medium and Nutr+ medium, indicating that communities can act cohesively under these conditions. We next asked whether parental species composition alone predicts which parental community will dominate after coalescence---and by how much. For each medium separately, we trained a logistic regression model that takes the two parent relative abundances as input and predicts a dominance score (Methods; \figref{fig:fig5}A). We evaluated performance in a leave-one-out scheme, selecting the model with the lowest test RMSE and using it to quantify predictability (Methods). Predictability varied significantly between the two media. In Nutr+ medium, predictability was high (\figref{fig:fig5}B, right; $R^2 = 0.912$), indicating that the species identities in each parental community are sufficient to predict which community is likely to dominate the coalescence. However, in Base medium, the model only somewhat captured the mean dominance (\figref{fig:fig5}B, left; $R^2 = 0.336$). These results indicate that in Nutr+ medium the strong communities are dominant because the species themselves are good competitors, whereas in Base medium the community-level selection arises from an emergent interaction within the parental communities and that the strong communities are dominant because the species form a good ``team''.

To explain the higher predictability observed in Nutr+ medium, we examined the linear coefficients of the fitted regression model and found that a small set of five species accounted for most of the predictive signal (Supplementary Fig.~S13). In Nutr+ medium, isolates with positive weights included ASV3 (Klebsiella), ASV8 (Lactococcus), and ASV12 (Chryseobacter), all of which acidify the medium in monoculture, whereas isolates with negative weights included ASV9 (Pedobacter) and ASV11 (Leuconostoc), which alkalize (\figref{fig:fig5}C). Consistent with the pH change in monoculture, the relative abundance of these species is highly correlated with the final pH of the parental community (Supplementary Fig.~S14). This pattern supports a pH-mediated mechanism: in Nutr+, communities enriched in acidifiers or depleted of alkalizers set a more acidic environment that tilts coalescence toward that parent, whereas a higher abundance of alkalizers shifts the pH upward and weakens or reverses this advantage. In line with this, the difference in parental community pH values explained the direction and magnitude of dominance in Nutr+ but not in Base (\figref{fig:fig5}D; $R^2 = 0.40$ vs $R^2 = 0.06$). These results indicate that under high nutrient supply, environmental modification via pH mediates community-level selection and renders outcomes more species-driven.

Taken together, our experiments reveal two regimes of community-level selection (\figref{fig:fig5}). In Nutr+, coalescence outcomes indicate a species-driven regime: a few high-impact, pH-modifying taxa largely determine the winner, yielding high predictability from species-level features (\figref{fig:fig5}B--D; \figref{fig:fig5}E right). In Base medium, coalescence outcomes suggest an emergent regime: CLS depends on multi-species, collectively mediated dynamics with weak species-level predictors (\figref{fig:fig5}B--D; \figref{fig:fig5}E left). This shift is not fully explained by a normalized gLV with unstructured random interactions; instead, we found that explaining the shift requires assuming that increasing interaction strength co-varies with a gradual rise in the variance of carrying capacities ($K$), introducing more hierarchical effective interactions (see Supplementary Information, Supplementary Fig.~S15). Together, these results highlight the versatile nature of dominance predictability in community coalescence---spanning species-level to community-level determination across environmental contexts.
