\begin{abstract}
A fundamental question in ecology is whether selection operates primarily at the species level or can emerge at the community level. Community coalescence, the mixing of two or more pre-assembled communities, provides a direct experimental test: if community-level selection occurs, one parental community should systematically dominate during mixing, with species from that parent collectively outcompeting those from the other parent. Here, we combine bacterial microcosm experiments with theoretical modeling to demonstrate that interspecies interaction strength governs both the occurrence and predictability of community-level selection. In initial experiments with Base medium with moderate interaction strength, we observe frequent \textit{community-level selection} (CLS) where one parental community often dominates the other. A generalized Lotka--Volterra model reproduces this frequent dominance and further predicts that selection weakens at low interaction strength and intensifies at higher interaction strength. Experiments that tune the nutrient concentration of the medium (a control knob for interspecies interaction strength) recapitulate this pattern, in line with the model's prediction. An analysis of predictability further reveals two regimes of community selection: a species-driven regime under strong interactions, where a few dominant species determine the winner, and an emergent regime under intermediate interactions, where higher-order interactions within the community collectively shape the outcome and reduce species-level predictability. Together, our results demonstrate that community-level selection emerges conditionally from interspecies interactions, offering a tractable framework for understanding and steering microbial coalescence.
\end{abstract}
