\begin{abstract}
It has long been debated in ecology whether communities behave as cohesive units or as loose collections of independent species. Here, we address this question through community coalescence, the mixing of previously isolated communities that were each independently assembled to equilibrium. Using bacterial microcosm experiments combined with ecological modeling, we show that interspecies interaction strength determines whether community-level selection occurs. When interactions are moderate to strong, community-level selection is common, with one parent community consistently outcompeting the other. In contrast, when interactions are weak, coalescence typically yields symmetric mixtures of the two communities, indicating the absence of community-level selection. These patterns generalize to natural sample-derived communities, which harbor greater taxonomic diversity and have undergone natural assembly processes distinct from controlled synthetic consortia. We further identify two distinct regimes underlying community-level selection: an emergent regime at intermediate interaction strength, in which collective dynamics shape outcomes that cannot be predicted from species traits alone, and a species-driven regime at strong interactions, where dominant species determine the winning community. Together, these results reconcile conflicting observations on community-level selection during community coalescence by demonstrating that only sufficiently strong interactions cause communities to behave as cohesive units of selection.
\end{abstract}
