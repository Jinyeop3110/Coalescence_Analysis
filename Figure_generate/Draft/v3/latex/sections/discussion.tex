\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our work demonstrates that community-level selection (CLS) predominantly shapes coalescence. Analyzing coalescence of pre-assembled communities both theoretically and experimentally, we found that in the presence of intermediate or strong interspecies interactions CLS was the most common outcome, with Mixture and Restructuring occurring less frequently. Theoretical modeling with a gLV framework predicts a shift from CLS to Mixture as interspecies interaction strength decreases. Varying nutrient concentration provided a single control knob for interaction strength, shifting outcome patterns in a predictable manner consistent with the model. Finally, species-level predictability revealed a two-regime view of community-level selection: a species-driven regime, where a few high-impact, pH-modifying taxa largely determine the winning community, and an emergent regime, where multi-species collective dynamics reduce predictability. Together, our work highlights the versatile and variable nature of community coalescence, while community-level selection and their predictability emerge conditionally.

In addition to prior studies, our results provide new insight into how communities behave as cohesive units. Theory based on consumer--resource models shows that communities can behave as quasi-units without cooperation and that ``community fitness'' can better predict survival than single-species traits \citep{Tikhonov2016}. Empirical work has shown that coalescence often exhibits coselection between dominant species and subdominant species, including both top-down and bottom-up manners \citep{DiazColunga2022}. It has also been shown that coalescence outcomes can depend on initial population sizes, reflecting resource-mediated propagule effects \citep{Goldman2025}. Complementing mechanistic models, our work demonstrates that a phenomenological gLV framework, seeded by random interspecies interactions, can yield cohesiveness and generate a rich distribution of outcomes, with predictable shifts toward CLS at moderate to high interaction strengths. Because the gLV framework aggregates interactions from multiple mechanisms---including pH modulation, metabolite toxicity, and resource competition---this perspective provides a general view on what constitutes a ``community'' under coalescence.

In nature, coalescence is contextualized in richer settings that add axes likely to shift regimes and outcomes. Abiotic heterogeneity, including temperature, buffering capacity, and other physicochemical factors, co-varies with interaction strength and can reshape the nature of coalescence cross environments \citep{Rillig2015, Castledine2020, Rillig2017}. In host-microbe systems (e.g.\ gut microbiome), host filtering, priority effects, and spatial structure further modulate which donor subcommunity engrafts, making coalescence a useful lens for assessing and steering engraftment \citep{Liu2024, Smillie2018}. Furthermore, our study does not consider the dynamics present in nature---e.g., fluctuating resources, migration pulses, or host responses---as we focus on steady-state outcomes after serial dilution. Looking ahead, incorporating these additional axes into both theory (resource- and pH-explicit models coupled to gLV) and experiment (systematic variation of inoculum ratios, multi-source mixtures, spatial structure, and longer evolutionary timescales) could build a more comprehensive picture of community-level selection across habitats and scales.
