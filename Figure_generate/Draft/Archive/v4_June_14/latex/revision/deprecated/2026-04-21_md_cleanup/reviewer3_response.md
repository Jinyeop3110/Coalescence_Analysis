## R3-1. Dimensionality artifact in similarity metrics (CRITICAL)

> *The key takeaway here is that, when using these similarity metrics and the author's "Dominance-Mixing-Restructuring" classification, one could expect to observe a decline in the "Dominance" fraction as community diversity increases due to a simple statistical effect: positive unit vectors are more likely to be close to orthogonal in lower dimensions than they are in higher dimensions.*
>
> *This effect is partially accounted for in the manuscript; specifically, Extended Data Fig. 3 shows that the empirical distribution of Dominance values has significantly higher mean than its counterparts generated by the null models. However, while the authors' claim is correct ("skewed abundance distributions alone do not fully account for the observed asymmetric outcomes"), this does not mean that the empirically quantified Dominance values aren't biased by the statistical effects described above. Many cases flagged as "Dominance" may in actuality be compatible with a simple null model, particularly for the communities assembled from the less diverse species pools. In my view, it would be necessary to compare each coalescence community with a corresponding null model constructed specifically for each of the pairs of parental communities, such as the simple additive model n_C,null = n_A + n_B. Comparing the experimental coalesced community with such a null model should be done on a case-by-case basis, not just by comparing distributions of Dominance indexes as in Extended Data Fig. 3.*

**Summary:** Dominance may be inflated by low-dimensional geometry. Need case-by-case comparison with additive null n_C,null = n_A + n_B, not just distributional comparison.

**Analysis result:** DONE (P3.4). This is the single most important new analysis. Key finding: the additive null n_C,null = normalize(n_A + n_B) universally predicts Mixture — 263 out of 263 events are classified as Mixture under the null. All 157 observed Dominance events therefore represent genuine departures from passive mixing (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴⁴). The nutrient-dependent shift from Mixture to Dominance is entirely absent in the null model.

**Figures:** `fig1_paired_classification.pdf`, `fig2_delta_PDI_histogram.pdf`, `fig3_contingency_heatmap.pdf`, `fig4_per_medium_panels.pdf`, `fig5_per_medium_contingency.pdf`, `fig6_asymmetricity_space.pdf`, `fig7_per_medium_delta_PDI.pdf`, `fig8_transition_diagram.pdf`

**Draft response text:**

We thank the reviewer for this important point about the geometric dependence of similarity metrics on dimensionality. We have performed the case-by-case comparison the reviewer suggests.

For each of the 263 coalescence events, we computed the additive null community n_C,null = normalize(n_A + n_B), which represents the expected composition under passive mixing without ecological dynamics. We then classified both the observed and null outcomes using the same similarity framework.

The result is unambiguous: the additive null universally predicts Mixture (263/263 events, 100%). This occurs because equal mixing of two communities with non-overlapping species produces a composition that is, by construction, equidistant from both parents in similarity space. In contrast, the observed outcomes show 157 Dominance, 84 Mixture, and 22 Restructuring events. The shift from null-expected Mixture to observed Dominance is highly significant (Wilcoxon signed-rank test on paired PDI values, p = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴⁴; new Supplementary Fig. [PLACEHOLDER]).

Crucially, the nutrient-dependent transition — from predominantly Mixture in Nutr− to predominantly Dominance in Nutr+ — is entirely absent from the null model, which predicts Mixture uniformly across all conditions. This demonstrates that the observed Dominance fraction reflects genuine ecological selection rather than geometric artifacts of dimensionality.

We also performed a mixing-ratio sweep (α · n_A + (1−α) · n_B for α ∈ [0, 1]) to show that the additive null at α = 0.5 trivially produces Mixture, and that observed communities are displaced toward the parental endpoints in a manner not captured by any linear mixing model (new Supplementary Fig. [PLACEHOLDER]).

The additive null uniformly predicts Mixture (263/263 events), whereas the observed outcomes include 157 Dominance events (Wilcoxon p = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴⁴). Moreover, the nutrient-dependent shift toward Dominance is absent from the null, confirming that ecological interactions, not geometric artifacts, drive the observed patterns."

## R3-2. Interaction strength, diversity, and Dominance frequency

> *In section 2.3 of the Results, the authors examine how varying the mean interaction (competition) strength between species in a gLV model affects coalescence outcomes. The main conclusion here is that increasing competition strength shifts the distribution of coalescence outcomes from all "Mixing" to mostly "Dominance" and "Restructuring." However, it is not clear to what extent this phenomenon can be explained by simple statistical effects like the ones described above. Increasing the mean interaction strength (mu) can be expected to decrease the number of species in the communities, and, as mentioned above, reducing N generically causes an increase in the frequency of "Dominance" outcomes. It seems important to test whether the increase of "Dominance" outcomes in the model (and in the experiments) as mu grows is substantially different from the expected increase in the null models as N decreases. It would also be useful to see how richness (in the final communities, not in the inocula) changes with mu in the model, as well as across the different media (Base, Nutr-, Nutr+) used in the experiment. From Supplementary Figs. 10-12, it seems like communities derived from natural samples do exhibit substantial diversity differences in the Base and Nutr+ media with respect to the Nutr- medium, but I could not find an equivalent representation for the first experiment.*

**Summary:** Is the Dominance increase with μ just an artifact of decreasing diversity? Show richness vs μ and vs media; compare observed Dominance to null expectation at matched diversity.

**Analysis results:**

*Experiments (P3.5):* Richness decreases with nutrient enrichment (parental communities: Nutr− median = 12, Nutr+ median = 7.5; p < 0.001). However, the Dominance shift (39% → 65% → 76%) is disproportionate to the richness change.

*Simulations (P4.1):* At high μ, compositional unevenness alone accounts for ~35% Dominance (from a null model that shuffles compositions while preserving unevenness). The observed Dominance is ~77%, yielding ~43 percentage points of excess Dominance attributable to ecological assembly structure. Excess Dominance correlates with μ (r = 0.695, p = 1.65 × 10⁻⁴). Mean excess for μ ≥ 0.3: 45.2 percentage points.

**Figures:** `richness_by_medium.pdf`, `richness_coalesced_vs_parental.pdf`, `richness_thresholds.pdf`, `richness_mu_analysis.pdf`, `richness_mu_stacked.pdf`

**Draft response text:**

We have performed the analysis the reviewer suggests, both for experiments and simulations.

**Experiments:** Species richness (ASVs above 0.1% threshold) decreases significantly with nutrient enrichment: parental communities have median richness of 12 (Nutr−), 9 (Base), and 7.5 (Nutr+) (Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.001; new Supplementary Fig. [PLACEHOLDER]). We note that coalesced communities follow a similar trend. However, the magnitude of Dominance increase (39% → 65% → 76%) is disproportionate to the richness decrease, suggesting that the diversity reduction alone does not account for the observed transition.

**Simulations:** To directly test the reviewer's concern, we constructed a composition-shuffling null model that preserves the unevenness structure of post-assembly communities at each μ but destroys the ecological information (species identity relative to community membership). At high μ, this null predicts ~35% Dominance from unevenness alone. The observed Dominance (~77%) exceeds this by ~43 percentage points — this "excess Dominance" is attributable to ecological assembly and increases with μ (Pearson r = 0.695, p = 1.65 × 10⁻⁴; new Supplementary Fig. [PLACEHOLDER]).

Furthermore, pairwise selection correlation (Extended Data Figs. 5–6) provides independent evidence that is not subject to the dimensionality artifact: correlated species fates within parental communities cannot arise from geometric effects on similarity metrics.

**Manuscript locations:**

Results §2.3, add after interaction strength sweep:

> "To test whether decreasing diversity with increasing μ could account for the rise in Dominance (a geometric effect of dimensionality on similarity metrics), we compared observed outcomes against a composition-shuffling null model that preserves unevenness but destroys ecological structure. At high μ, unevenness alone predicts ~35% Dominance, whereas the observed ~77% yields ~43 percentage points of excess attributable to ecological assembly (Supplementary Fig. [X])."

Results §2.4, add richness data for experiments:

> "Species richness decreased with nutrient enrichment (median parental richness: 12, 9, and 7.5 ASVs for Nutr−, Base, and Nutr+, respectively; Supplementary Fig. [Y]), but the magnitude of Dominance increase exceeded what richness reduction alone would predict."

## R3-3. gLV limited to competitive interactions

> *With respect to the model, the gLV framework is restricted to competitive interactions (alpha_ij > 0). While this is reasonable from a modeling perspective because it prevents situations of "unchecked growth," it also limits the range of ecological scenarios that the model can represent. Previous works have shown that facilitative interactions (particularly in the form of metabolic cross-feeding) are an important determinant of correlated species' fates during coalescence. This seems particularly relevant given that "Restructuring" appears to be much more common in natural sample-derived communities than in the initial bottom-up-assembled communities. Given these discrepancies, and the fact that natural sample-derived communities are still model systems (even if they may be closer to natural microbiomes than communities assembled from defined species pools), I would suggest the claims in section 2.6 of the Results as well as in the Discussion are toned down (e.g., lines 292-294).*

**Summary:** gLV lacks facilitation. Natural communities have more Restructuring, possibly from cross-feeding. Tone down claims.

**Analysis result:** Text revisions applied (P1.8).

**Draft response text:**

We agree that the competition-only gLV model cannot capture facilitative interactions such as metabolic cross-feeding, which may be particularly important in natural communities with greater metabolic diversity. The higher Restructuring frequency observed in natural communities (compared to synthetic communities and model predictions) is consistent with this limitation.

We have toned down the claims in §2.6 and the Discussion:

- Lines 292–294: Changed from "These results demonstrate that community-level selection is a robust phenomenon" to "These results suggest that the qualitative trend of increasing Dominance with nutrient concentration extends beyond precisely controlled synthetic consortia."

- Added: "The higher Restructuring frequency in natural communities may reflect facilitative interactions such as metabolic cross-feeding, or more complex interaction networks not captured by our competition-only gLV model."

- Discussion: Added acknowledgment that "systems with substantial mutualism or facilitation may exhibit qualitatively different dynamics."

**Manuscript locations:** Results §2.6 and Discussion. DONE in P1.8.

## R3-4. "Interaction strength" vs "competition strength" terminology

> *One last comment along these lines, which is admittedly mostly semantic: the term "interaction strength" may be somewhat confusing since, in the context of the gLV model, it refers strictly to competitive interactions. Would two species which engage in a mutualism be considered "weak interactors" or the opposite? It may be worth considering using different terminology ("competition strength"?)*

**Summary:** "Interaction strength" is ambiguous when the model only has competition.

**Analysis result:** Text clarification applied (P1.4).

**Draft response text:**

We appreciate this terminological concern. We have chosen to retain "interaction strength" for two reasons: (1) it is the standard terminology in the community ecology literature (May 1972; Allesina & Tang 2012; Grilli et al. 2017; Hu et al. 2022), and (2) in the experimental context, our proxy (failed invasion frequency) integrates over all interaction types, not just pairwise competition.

To avoid confusion, we have added an explicit disambiguation in Results §2.2:

> "Throughout the text, we use 'interaction strength' as shorthand for this mean competition coefficient μ when referring to the gLV model; in the experimental context, we use the term more broadly to denote the overall intensity of competitive interactions, as proxied by the frequency of failed pairwise invasions."

This sentence already existed in the manuscript but we have verified its placement is prominent and clear.

**Manuscript location:** Results §2.2, after Eq. 2. DONE in P1.4.
